School Clubs & Movements

Debating Club

Learning to think clearly and to speak with confidence.

A debate is an organised discussion in which two teams argue for and against an idea. That idea is called the motion. The Debating Club trains pupils to speak clearly, to give good reasons for what they believe, to listen carefully and to respect people who think differently. It is one of the best ways to build confidence in spoken English and to sharpen critical thinking.

Debating is not about shouting or winning at all costs. It is about preparing well, organising ideas, speaking politely and learning from others. Pupils who debate become better speakers, better listeners and better thinkers.

At a glance

Teams
The Proposition (for the motion) and the Opposition (against it)
People in a debate
Chairperson, timekeeper, speakers, judges and an audience
Judged on
Matter (what you say), manner (how you say it) and method (how it is organised)
Language
Usually English, the language of instruction in school
Sample motion
This house believes that reading is more useful than playing games

What the club aims to do

  • Develop public speaking skills and the courage to speak before an audience.
  • Teach pupils to build an argument with reasons and examples.
  • Strengthen listening, note-taking and quick thinking.
  • Improve spoken and written English, and widen vocabulary.
  • Teach respect for other people’s opinions.

Debating words explained

Motion
The statement to be debated, for example “This house believes that homework is useful.”
The house
All the people present at the debate, including the audience.
Proposition
The team that supports the motion. It is sometimes called the Government.
Opposition
The team that disagrees with the motion.
Chairperson
The person who leads the debate, calls speakers in turn and keeps order. Speakers address the chair politely.
Timekeeper
The person who keeps time and signals when a speaker is near the end of his or her time.
Rebuttal
Answering and challenging the other side’s arguments.
Point of information
A short question or comment from the other side during a speech. It is allowed only if the speaker agrees to accept it.
Adjudicator
A judge who listens to the debate, gives marks and explains the decision.

How a debate runs

  1. The chairperson welcomes everyone, announces the motion and explains the rules.
  2. The first speaker of the Proposition explains what the motion means and presents the team’s main arguments.
  3. The first speaker of the Opposition responds and presents the Opposition’s main arguments.
  4. The second speakers of each side answer the other team and add new arguments.
  5. Summing-up speakers of each side remind the house of the strongest points of their case. They do not introduce completely new arguments.
  6. The chairperson opens the floor so that members of the audience can speak for or against the motion.
  7. The judges announce the result and give helpful feedback to every speaker.

How to build a speech

A good speech has an introduction, a body and a conclusion. In the introduction the speaker greets the chair and the house, states the side he or she is on and previews the main points. In the body, each argument is built with four steps, remembered as P-E-E-L:

  • Point: state the idea clearly in one sentence.
  • Explain: give the reason why the point is true.
  • Example: give a real example from school, home, the community or the news.
  • Link: connect the point back to the motion.

In the conclusion the speaker summarises the arguments and finishes strongly. For example, a speaker supporting the motion on reading might say: “Reading is useful (point) because it teaches us new words and ideas (explain). A pupil who reads every day usually writes better compositions (example). Therefore reading is more useful than playing games (link).”

What members do

  • Practise short speaking games, such as speaking for one minute on a surprise topic.
  • Research motions using library books, newspapers and discussions with teachers.
  • Practise taking notes while others speak.
  • Take part in prepared debates and in impromptu debates, where the motion is given only a few minutes before.
  • Argue a side they do not personally agree with, which teaches them to understand other points of view.
  • Debate between classes and houses, and between schools when invitations are available.
  • Take part in mock parliament sessions, where members act as the speaker, ministers and members.
  • Practise recitation, storytelling and presentations.

Rules and good manners

  • Speak only when the chairperson calls you.
  • Address the chair and the house politely.
  • Do not interrupt, except with a polite point of information that the speaker has agreed to accept.
  • Attack ideas, never people. Insults and unkind remarks are never allowed.
  • Keep to your time.
  • Listen with respect when others speak, and accept the judges’ decision graciously.

Examples of motions

The motions are suitable for the age of the pupils and always related to everyday life. Examples include:

  • This house believes that reading is more useful than playing games.
  • This house believes that children should help with household work.
  • This house believes that education is more valuable than money.
  • This house believes that boarding school is better than day school.
  • This house believes that every school should have a garden.
  • This house believes that pupils should wear school uniform.

Skills and values pupils gain

  • Confidence in speaking before others.
  • Clear thinking and the ability to give reasons.
  • Organisation of ideas, which helps in composition writing too.
  • Careful listening and respect for different views.
  • Preparation, discipline and teamwork.

Who can join, and how

Any interested pupil may join, including pupils who are shy. Older pupils often take the main speaking roles in debates, while younger pupils join the practice games and contribute from the floor. The teacher in charge groups members so that every pupil has a role that suits his or her level.

For meeting days, times and any contributions, please ask the teacher in charge or the school office.

Frequently asked questions

My child is shy. Will debating be too hard?

Many members begin shy. Pupils start with short, easy speaking games in small groups, and the club is a safe place to practise. Confidence grows step by step.

Must pupils defend ideas they disagree with?

Sometimes. Arguing a side you do not personally hold is a useful exercise. It teaches pupils to understand other points of view, and everyone knows it is a speaking exercise rather than a personal opinion.

Will debating help my child’s school results?

It strengthens spoken English, vocabulary, reasoning, note-taking and organisation of ideas, all of which help with comprehension, composition and oral work.

Is debating always a competition?

Debates may be judged, but the main purpose is learning. Judges give feedback so that every speaker knows how to improve.

Which language is used?

Debates are usually held in English, so that pupils practise the language of their examinations.

The Debating Club helps pupils to find their voice, to use it wisely and to respect the voices of others. These are skills that will serve them in school, at work and in public life.
